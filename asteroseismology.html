Échelle Diagram

How does an échelle diagram work?

In a Sun-like star the peaks repeat in a regular pattern: roughly every Δν (the "large separation") you meet another l = 0 peak, another l = 1 peak, and so on. That spacing is set by how long sound takes to cross the star, so it tells us the star's density.

So we cut the spectrum into strips of width Δν and stack them like lines of text, lowest frequency at the bottom. If the strip width is exactly Δν, peaks of the same l land in the same column and form vertical ridges. If it is too narrow or too wide, each strip is shifted a little from the one below, and the ridges lean. Finding the width that makes them straight is how you measure Δν. (Échelle is French for "ladder".)

Reading the plot below: the horizontal axis is position within a strip (frequency modulo Δν), the vertical axis is the frequency where each strip starts, and brighter colour means more power.

Light Curve

The raw data: how the star's brightness changes over time, in parts per million (ppm). The yellow curve is the sum of all the oscillation modes. The grey curve adds the slower flicker from granulation (convection cells boiling at the surface), which is what a telescope actually records. You can't pick out individual frequencies by eye, which is why we use the power spectrum above.

What is oscillating?

A star is a ball of gas that can resonate. Near-surface convection churns like a pot of boiling water, randomly kicking the star's resonant modes, which ring and fade over days to weeks. For the Sun, the strongest modes have periods around five minutes, far too low for us to hear, but the physics is the same as an organ pipe.

Most modes we observe in Sun-like stars and red giants are p modes (pressure is the restoring force, so they are sound waves). Deep in the core, g modes (buoyancy is the restoring force) can also exist. Each mode is labelled by its radial order n (nodes along the radius), degree l (total nodal lines on the surface), and azimuthal order m (nodal lines through the poles).

Reading the spectrum: Δν and νmax

High-order p modes follow a nearly regular pattern, νn,l ≈ Δν (n + l/2 + ε) − δν0l. The large separation Δν is the inverse of the time sound takes to cross the star and back, which scales with its mean density: Δν ∝ (M/R3)1/2. The frequency of maximum power νmax tracks the acoustic cutoff frequency near the surface and scales with surface gravity: νmax ∝ g/√Teff. Two numbers, two unknowns, so we can solve for M and R.

The small separation: a view of the core

Modes with l = 0 and l = 2 travel along nearly the same path, except near the centre. Their small frequency difference δν02 is sensitive to conditions in the core, which change as hydrogen is converted to helium. It is one of the best age indicators we have for Sun-like stars. Use the "Zoom on one order" view to see it.

The Sun: the star we can resolve

For distant stars we see only the total light from an unresolved disk. Bright and dark patches of high-l modes cancel, so only l ≤ 3 survive. The Sun is different: we image its surface directly and measure Doppler shifts pixel by pixel, which reveals millions of modes up to l of several thousand. That is helioseismology, and it mapped the Sun's internal sound speed and rotation in detail. This lab shows the Sun as it would look from far away so you can compare it with the other stars.

Red giants and mixed modes

In red giants the dense helium core supports g modes whose frequencies overlap the p modes. They couple, and each dipole p mode is replaced by a cluster of mixed modes that behave like sound waves in the envelope and like gravity waves in the core. Their period spacing ΔΠ1 tells stars still climbing the red-giant branch (about 60 to 90 s) apart from helium-burning red-clump stars (about 250 to 350 s), even when they look identical on the surface. Because g-dominated modes feel the core, their rotational splittings showed that red-giant cores spin much faster than their envelopes.

What this model simplifies

Frequencies come from the asymptotic relation with curvature, not from solving the full stellar oscillation equations. The ray diagram uses a schematic Sun-like sound-speed profile scaled to the star's mass and radius. Amplitudes, linewidths, granulation, and noise follow approximate empirical scalings. The scaling relations themselves are accurate to a few percent for real stars. The listening mode speeds the oscillations up so that νmax lands on 440 Hz.
