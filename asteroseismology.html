Asteroseismology Lab

Stars like the Sun vibrate. Convection near the surface constantly drives sound waves, and because those waves are trapped inside the star, it rings at a set of specific harmonics. Each vibration makes the star brighten and dim very slightly. In this lab you measure those harmonics from the star's light and use them to find the star's mass and radius.

1Observe the Light Curve

A telescope records how bright the star is over time. This record is called a light curve. Each sound wave inside the star squeezes and heats the photosphere, then lets it expand and cool. Hotter gas glows brighter, so the star's brightness rises and falls by a few ppm.

The telescope also picks up two larger sources of variation that have nothing to do with the vibrations. Granulation is the flicker of convection cells boiling at the surface. Photon noise is random scatter in how much light arrives each moment.

2Transform into a Power Spectrum

A Fourier transform breaks the light curve into the separate frequencies it contains, the same way the Sound Spectrum Analyzer breaks a musical note into its harmonics. The result is a power spectrum. Each harmonic of the star becomes one narrow peak. Granulation and photon noise spread across every frequency as a low background. Click any peak to see its single wave in the light curve above.

Frequency resolution 1/T =

T is the total length of the observation, not the period of a wave. Two peaks closer together than 1/T blur into one. Shorten T and watch the peaks merge and sink into the noise. This is why Kepler watched the same stars for four years.

Frequency (μHz)
Peaks. Each peak is one harmonic. Its color shows the surface pattern, set by the degree l. The n labels below the axis number the harmonics.
Δν, the large separation. The spacing between neighboring peaks of the same color. Like the v/2L spacing of a guitar string's harmonics, it is one over the time sound takes to cross the star and back. It measures the star's mean density.
νmax. The frequency where the peaks are tallest. It measures the star's surface gravity.
δν02, the small separation. The narrow gap between a green l = 2 peak and the blue l = 0 peak beside it. It depends on the core, so it changes as the star ages.
Background. The low, uneven floor comes from granulation and photon noise. Noise also makes each peak a little taller or shorter at random.
Your measurements. The white spacing lines are set one Δν apart, using your value. The yellow line shows your νmax.

These are the only two measurements you need.

Astronomers measure Δν to about 1% and νmax to about 3 to 5%. The checks above use the same targets.

4Infer the Star's Properties

The scaling relations turn your two measurements into the star's density and gravity, and from those its radius and mass. Everything is compared with the Sun.

Your Δν
→
Mean density
ρ / ρSun = (Δν / 135.1 μHz)²

Your νmax
→
Surface gravity
g / gSun = (νmax / 3090 μHz) × (T / 5772 K)1/2

Gravity depends on M/R² and density on M/R³. Combining them gives

Radius
R = g ÷ ρ
Mass
M = g³ ÷ ρ²

Mass depends on Δν to the −4 power, so a 1% error in Δν becomes a 4% error in mass. These relations are calibrated on the Sun and are accurate to a few percent. Kepler and TESS have used them to measure tens of thousands of stars.

5See the Pattern on the Surface

Each peak matches a vibration pattern on the star's surface, shown here hugely exaggerated. Orange regions move outward and blue regions move inward. The degree l is the number of nodal lines. Drag to rotate.

Degree l

A distant star appears as a single point, so we only see the combined light of its whole face. The bright and dark patches of high-l patterns cancel out, which leaves only l = 0 to 3 detectable. The Sun is the exception, because we can image its surface directly.

6See What Each Harmonic Probes

Sound travels faster in the hot interior than near the cooler surface. A wave heading inward at an angle bends back outward through refraction, then reflects off the surface. Each harmonic is trapped between the surface and its turning point, shown as a dashed circle.
